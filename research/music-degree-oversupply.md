# Are universities over-producing music graduates?

**Question:** In the USA (and the wider Anglosphere, data permitting), by what factor are
universities currently over-producing music-degree graduates, compared to market demand?

**Status:** Research notes, compiled 2026-08-30. All figures sourced below; figures I derived
myself are labelled **[derived]** and should not be cited as if they were published statistics.

---

## Headline finding

**There is no single honest number.** The answer swings from ~1× to ~69× depending entirely on
what you count as "demand" — and that sensitivity is the finding, not an evasion of it.

| If "demand" means…                                        | Oversupply factor | Confidence |
| --------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------- | ---------- |
| Any paid musical work, part-time and intermittent included  | **~1.1×**         | Sourced    |
| A full-time living earned primarily from music              | **~5–8×**         | **[derived]** |
| The job conservatories nominally train for (salaried orchestra seat) | **~69×**  | Sourced    |
| Teaching music in K–12 schools                              | **<1× (shortage)**| Sourced    |

The most defensible one-sentence formulation:

> US institutions produce music-**performance** graduates at roughly 5–10× the rate the market
> creates full-time performing livelihoods — rising to ~69× for the salaried orchestral positions
> the conservatory model was built around — while simultaneously **under**-producing the music
> teachers the school system actually demands.

The oversupply is a *performance-track* phenomenon, not a music-degree phenomenon per se. Any
analysis that treats "music degrees" as one undifferentiated category will get the answer wrong.

## 1. Supply: what US institutions produce

| Measure                                                  | Figure     | Year    |
| -------------------------------------------------------- | ---------- | ------- |
| All music degrees (CIP 50.09), every level                | **25,860** | 2021–22 |
| — Bachelor's in music                                     | ~15,839    | 2019–20 |
| — of which, music **performance** bachelor's              | ~7,281     | recent  |
| — Master's in music                                       | 6,247      | 2021–22 |
| — Doctorates (music + music education)                    | ~1,200/yr  | recent  |
| Music **education** bachelor's (CIP 13.1312, counted separately under Education) | ~3,393 | recent |

An independent IPEDS reconstruction covering **362,000+ music-related degree completions between
2011 and 2024 across 1,500+ institutions** implies ~26,000/year, consistent with the table above.

Two counting traps worth flagging:

- **Music education sits in a different CIP code** (13.1312, under Education) than music proper
  (50.09). Analyses that use only CIP 50.09 silently omit the one segment where demand is healthy.
- Music is the **43rd most popular major** nationally — large in absolute terms, but not an
  outlier field. The problem is the narrowness of the target occupation, not the volume of majors.

## 2. Demand: what the US market absorbs

| Occupation (BLS, 2024–34 projections)   | Employment | Growth | Openings/yr |
| ---------------------------------------- | ---------- | ------ | ----------- |
| Musicians and singers                    | 169,800    | +1%    | ~19,400     |
| Music directors and composers            | —          | ~0%    | ~4,300      |
| **Total**                                |            |        | **~23,700** |

Median hourly wage for musicians and singers: **$42.45** (May 2024) — a healthy *rate*, but the
hours are not there. BLS notes part-time work is common and that many musicians "find only
part-time or intermittent work and may have long periods of unemployment between jobs."

### Why ~23,700 openings vs ~25,900 degrees (≈1.1×) is a misleading comparison

Four structural reasons this apparent near-balance overstates how much room exists for graduates:

1. **Almost none of it is growth.** At +1% per decade, essentially every "opening" is replacement
   churn — people leaving an occupation they could not live on. Counting exits as opportunities
   treats the field's failure to retain people as evidence of its health.
2. **A BLS "job" is not a living.** The 169,800 figure counts part-time and intermittent gig work
   at the same weight as salaried positions.
3. **No degree is required.** Graduates compete for these openings against non-graduates, so
   degree production and openings are not two sides of one ledger.
4. **Stock, not just flow.** Each year's cohort joins a standing pool of earlier cohorts still
   trying. The orchestral job search is realistically modelled as a **~10-year window** after the
   bachelor's, spent on auditions, fellowships, freelancing, further study, and part-time
   orchestras. So ~15 cohorts may be competing for one year's openings.

## 3. The orchestral track: the clean, citable extreme

| Quantity                                   | Figure  |
| ------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Full-time salaried US orchestra positions   | ~4,000  |
| Assumed annual turnover                     | ~3%     |
| Implied openings per year                   | ~120    |
| Orchestral-track graduates per year          | ~8,253  |
| **Graduates per opening**                    | **~69** |

Or, in the source's own framing: American higher education produces enough orchestral-track
graduates to **replace the entire salaried orchestra workforce about every two years.**
Postings routinely draw 200+ résumés for a single seat.

Two caveats, both of which make the real ratio *worse*, not better:

- Many advertised positions are filled by musicians **already employed in other orchestras moving
  laterally**, so counting advertised openings overstates opportunity for new graduates.
- The 3% turnover assumption is deliberately conservative; orchestral seats are famously held for
  decades.

This is not a new or cyclical condition. The 28-year "Musical Chairs" study found performance
graduates rising steadily for decades into a market with *decreasing* job opportunities.

## 4. The counter-current: music education is short, not long

This is the most commonly missed part of the picture, and it cuts against the oversupply thesis:

- **~9,533** active music-educator job openings in the US, against **140,000+** positions.
- Music education graduates have **fallen 14% over the past decade** (National Association of
  Schools of Music data).
- Several states report outright **shortages**; experienced teachers have exited at an accelerated
  rate post-pandemic and are being replaced by inexperienced or uncertified staff.

So the same higher-education system is *over*-producing performers and *under*-producing teachers
at the same time. That is an allocation failure inside music schools, not a volume failure.

## 5. The contrary evidence (stated fairly)

The strongest published pushback on "oversupply" comes from **SNAAP** (Strategic National Arts
Alumni Project, 16,317 respondents across 105 institutions):

- **61%** of music performance majors currently work as professional artists; **82%** have done so.
- Only **2.3%** reported being unemployed and looking for work (Fall 2011) — below the
  then-prevailing general unemployment rate.

Why this does not settle the question: "works as a professional artist" counts *any* paid artistic
work, however marginal or part-time; the data is self-reported by voluntary respondents (a survey
of alumni reachable through their institutions under-samples those who left the field entirely);
and low unemployment among people who are largely self-employed measures something different from
whether the work sustains a living. It is real evidence that music graduates are employable — it is
weak evidence that they are employed *as musicians, full-time*.

## 6. The derived middle estimate **[derived — not a published statistic]**

To bridge "any paid gig" (~1.1×) and "orchestral seat" (~69×), here is an explicit model of
full-time performing livelihoods. **The assumptions are mine and are the weak link:**

```
BLS musician/singer jobs                             169,800
× share representing full-time-equivalent livelihoods    25–35%   ← ASSUMPTION
= full-time musical livelihoods                       42,000–59,000
× annual replacement rate                                 ~5%     ← ASSUMPTION
= genuine full-time openings per year                  2,100–3,000

Music bachelor's per year (excl. music education)        ~12,400
→ oversupply factor                                       ~4–6×
Using all music bachelor's (~15,800)                      ~5–8×
```

Sanity check on the 25–35% full-time share: the UK reports ~54,300 *working musicians* against
~157,800 *music creators* (~34%), which is the same order of magnitude.

Treat this band as an informed estimate, not a finding. If it needs to survive fact-checking, cite
the 69:1 orchestral figure and the raw BLS-vs-IPEDS counts instead.

## 7. Anglosphere comparison

### United Kingdom — same structure, correction already underway

Earnings from the first-ever **Musicians' Census** (2023, ~6,000 respondents):

| Measure                                          | Figure  |
| ------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Average annual income *from music*                | £20,700 |
| Share earning **under £14,000/yr** from music     | **43%** |
| Average for those earning exclusively from music  | ~£30,000 |
| UK median income (comparison)                     | £33,280 |
| UK average for a working-age graduate (comparison)| £38,500 |
| Do not earn enough to support self/family         | 23%     |
| Cite lack of sustainable income as a career barrier| 44%    |

Market size: ~54,300 working musicians (Q1 2026); 157,800 "music creators"; 220,000 FTE roles
across the whole music sector. **70%** of professional UK musicians hold a degree and **50%** hold
a *music* degree — meaningfully higher credentialisation than the US, where no degree is required.

The notable difference from the US is that **the UK supply side is already contracting**, for
reasons mostly unrelated to labour-market signals:

- Music departments closing (University of Kent, Oxford Brookes).
- The school pipeline is collapsing beneath the universities: state schools and colleges offering
  A-Level Music **fell over 38% between 2010 and 2018**.
- Undergraduate fees frozen at £9,250 since 2017, eroding departmental finances in real terms.
- Russell Group music cohorts are flat-to-declining: 3,200 (2019–20) → 3,340 → 3,400 → 3,250
  (2022–23) across 19 institutions, averaging ~171 per institution.

### Canada — officially assessed as *balanced*

This is the most important counterpoint in the whole file. Canada's Occupational Projection System
(COPS) projects for musicians and singers, 2024–2033:

| Measure                | Figure |
| ----------------------- | ------ |
| Job openings            | 17,100 |
| Job seekers             | 18,100 |
| **Ratio**               | **~1.06×** |
| Annual employment growth| 1.1% (vs 1.2% national) |

COPS explicitly finds labour supply sufficient to meet demand over 2021–2023. Crucially, **57% of
Canadian musicians and singers work in colleges, CEGEPs and vocational schools** — i.e. the market
clears through *teaching*, which is precisely the mechanism the US data also points to.

### Australia — qualitative only, no ratio published

*Making Music Work: Sustainable Portfolio Careers for Australian Musicians* (QUT / ARC Linkage,
2015–19; 592 surveyed musicians + 11 interviews) finds the same lived reality — portfolio careers,
self-employment as the most common mode (~half of responses), difficulty earning a liveable wage,
and the need to avoid "over-gigging" to protect long-term career viability — but publishes **no
supply/demand ratio**. Do not source a factor to it.

## 8. What I would actually conclude

1. **Reject the premise of a single factor.** The question "by what factor" presupposes a
   well-defined denominator that does not exist. Report the range and the definitional dependency.
2. **The defensible claim is narrow and strong:** performance and orchestral training is
   over-produced by something between 5× and 69×, depending on how narrowly you define the target
   job. The 69:1 orchestral ratio is the most rigorously sourced number in this file.
3. **The system is simultaneously under-producing music teachers.** Any policy conclusion that
   simply says "fewer music degrees" would make the actual shortage worse.
4. **Teaching is the market-clearing mechanism**, visible in both the US shortage data and the
   Canadian 57%-in-education figure. Performance degrees are, in practice, substantially funnelling
   into teaching careers they were not designed to prepare people for.
5. **Credential inflation is doing real damage at the graduate level.** ~6,247 master's and ~1,200
   doctorates per year, chasing ~120 orchestral openings, is where the human cost concentrates —
   these are the students paying the most for the longest odds.

### Open questions / gaps in this research

- No direct IPEDS pull was possible (`nces.ed.gov`, `datausa.io`, `bls.gov` are blocked by this
  environment's egress proxy); degree counts come from secondary reporting of IPEDS data and
  should be re-verified against the primary tables before publication.
- The 25–35% full-time-equivalent share (§6) is the single weakest assumption here and deserves a
  proper source.
- No usable Australian or New Zealand supply-side degree counts were found.
- SNAAP's headline outcome figures date from 2011–2013 surveys and may not reflect the
  post-streaming, post-pandemic market.
