# How to Command Belief Like a Cult Leader

**Joanna Wiebe**, Copyhackers — [youtu.be/Tnv9PuMo84E](https://youtu.be/Tnv9PuMo84E)

---

Harley-Davidson, Lululemon, even Berkshire Hathaway operate like cults. Some of the world's most respected brands run like the world's most infamous cults, and are able to convince people to give up their money, their time, and their sense of choice.

Consider this. Every year, 40,000 people pilgrimage to Omaha and line up before dawn to see a man they call the Oracle. There's merch. Only a select few are invited in. And the Oracle's shareholder letters are quoted like scripture. This is not a traditional cult leader. It's Warren Buffett, billionaire investor.

Brands with a cult following exploit a deep psychological truth that most of us completely ignore: humans are starving for something to believe in. Your brand, big or small, can actively manufacture this belief through proven congregation-building principles you've probably never considered in your content strategy. So if you want to learn how to command belief in your brand so people defend you passionately and buy from you exclusively, let's do like Warren Buffett and take a page from what cults get right.

## A Shiny New Lens

The first secret of belief is what we're calling a shiny new lens, and it has nothing to do with the ins and outs of the product you're selling. It's all about how companies get you to view — not the product — the world.

That is how Simon Sinek explains why Apple became so successful. Steve Jobs sold a new way of looking at the world: to see beautiful design and breakthrough creativity where others could only see a dull gray box that did calculations fast. Once you put on those shiny new lenses, that boring box on your desk becomes downright offensive to your soul. All because you were handed a pair of new eyes.

You see the same concept with new brands, too. Fishwife sells canned fish, which should be the most boring thing on the shelf. Instead, they hand their fans the eyes of someone who treats tinned fish like a little luxury — a thing with a ritual around it, a thing you flex. It's still fish in a can, but you're seeing it completely differently because Fishwife is showing you a new way to look at it. Then there's RAADfest, which literally registered a nonprofit called the Church of Perpetual Life. The glasses they hand you say aging and death are not your fate; they're a disease you get to fight. People pay to show up at their huge events because they want to see the world that way, and the brand keeps growing.

This is how cults get started. They show you the world as you see it, or aspire to see it. Take famous cult leader Jim Jones in the 1950s and '60s. He built the Peoples Temple by handing people who'd been shoved to the edges of society something they'd never had: a place where every color person sat together, ate together, and mattered. Obviously, things went dark. What's curious to us in business is that survivors said the cult made them feel seen for the first time in their lives. They joined because Jim Jones gave them a new way of seeing.

When marketers try to start with why, they treat an innate human need to belong as a nice-to-have. Science proves it's a must-have. In 1995, two psychologists, Baumeister and Leary, found that a sense of belonging ranks right next to food and safety as a basic human need. We are wired to find a group. We're not *like* pack animals — we *are* pack animals. So as a brand, stop talking about building a community. Communities are wastelands in Circle or Slack. Community is not the goal. Congregation is the goal: a growing group of people who can't unsee what you've shown them, and who identify differently because of your brand's message.

If you're resisting this a bit, let me make you really uncomfortable. First, thinking this is dark-hat stuff that only the worst brands would ever do is naive. This is a clever, data-backed growth strategy that smart companies use to bury the brands who think they're above it. Second, your audience is not too sophisticated to fall for belonging. It's not only people from the fringes who join cults — researcher Margaret Singer found that about two-thirds of people who join cults come from normal, functioning families and upbringings.

When you apply this stuff, you don't have to go dark with it. Take the lesson, show your customers the world through a new lens, and roll with that in a positive way. Your customer is shopping for a worldview. They will buy it from you or your competitor.

To put this in practice, say this out loud in public over and over until people repeat it back to you, and then double down: *Most people think [blank]. Here's what's actually true.* Most people think canned fish is for grandma's pantry — here's what's actually true. Most people think computers are ugly plastic boxes — here's what's actually true. Most people think you have to be a lone wolf to be a rebel — here's what's actually true.

Nest did this by turning an ugly thermostat into something a design lover just had to have on their walls. Just three years after hitting the market with their lens on the design of things, Nest sold to Google for $3.2 billion in cash. So go find the lens your congregation needs from you.

## A Devil to Fight

Once you've given people a new way to see themselves and the world, you've got to give them something to fight against. The second secret of generating belief in you is identifying a devil that's fighting you. Every church, religion, and ideology has one, and so does every brand that people believe in. This is not a coincidence. It is entirely by design.

Do you remember Apple's iconic 1984 commercial, with the rebel cutting through drones to throw a hammer through a screen? Of course you do. It is a masterclass in a brand creating a devil its customers do battle with every time they buy from the brand. Apple painted IBM as Big Brother, the enemy, and in contrast they were then the rebellious savior. Two decades later, Apple did it all over again — same playbook. Mac versus PC, good versus evil, just evil in a cheap brown suit this time. If you think about it, Apple barely even talked about Apple. They pointed at who you didn't want to be, and you nodded along because you agreed who the enemy was.

Patagonia has pulled this same concept off without a competitor at all. Their devil is mindless consumerism, so they ran a Black Friday ad telling you not to buy their jacket — a premium brand built on an enemy that their followers could agree with.

This is just textbook cult mechanics. Leaders pull followers together by convincing them that they're better than outsiders, and that bond gets stronger the second there's an us versus them. From early on, L. Ron Hubbard, the Scientology guy, pointed his followers at one enemy. He called psychiatrists "the psychs" and painted them as the great evil of the planet. The church even runs a museum called *Psychiatry: An Industry of Death*. Notice the enemy isn't a person — it's a whole profession. Even people who leave Scientology struggle to trust psychiatry after. The devil feels that real.

That's the power of the devil narrative that runs through human history. Isn't the devil's secret power that he convinces you he doesn't exist? Isn't the devil good at tempting you? If you say there is no devil, isn't that the devil winning? Customers are good, followers are better, and followers who think your competitor is the worst are the best.

Here's the proof. In 2001, two researchers found that communities hold together around a shared adversary, and their main example was literally Mac versus PC. A clear enemy turns customers into followers, and followers behave differently. A customer leaves you for a 10%-off coupon; a follower defends you in the comments.

Brands get this wrong in two ways. First, they try to be everything for everyone, because "all are welcome" feels human, and human is probably one of your brand values. But an enemy makes deciding easier. That is the contrast effect at play — people compare and contrast to make decisions, and if we can easily eliminate one option, the devil, you can decide faster to choose you. The other mistake is making the enemy a person. You do not want to pick on a human. Pick a behavior, a belief, a broken status quo, or even a lazy institution. You're not a schoolground bully; you're the savior against a devil.

To try this, consider that if your brand stands for something, then it stands against what runs counter to it. If you're a perfume brand and you stand for custom scents tailored to the individual's chemical makeup, then you stand against one-size-fits-all. One-size-fits-all becomes evil. Complete the line: *We exist because the normal way of doing [blank] is broken, and we refuse to do it.*

The villain already exists for your customer. You just see it better, and the lens you gave them in step one helps them see it too. You're not inventing a devil; you're identifying that it exists, and you're fighting it. Not passively, either. You cannot build a congregation of wildly enthusiastic followers by kind of, sort of, maybe fighting something that feels a bit like a sort of devil — but that's a harsh word, so let's just dilute it. No. This work takes courage, for you and for your followers, because next they're going to have to show the world which side they're on.

## A Symbol You Wear

The third secret of belief in your brand is a symbol you wear. A symbol is the single most effective badge of honor, and it's a recruiting tool. If you think you've never bought such a symbol, think again. Symbols are a physical representation of who you are, what you stand for, and even how you behave, and they come in all different forms.

When I was a kid, I lived in a town with a lot of Mormons. I wasn't Mormon. I got to school one Monday and the girls in my class were all wearing these little bracelets with a light built into them. Most of my friends had them on. I wanted one — like, I needed one. When I asked them, "Where did you get that?" they were like, "At church." And I was like, "Tell me more about this church." That conversation was not an accident of the Mormon church.

Heaven's Gate is an eerie example, but worth learning from because it proves the power of symbols to unite a congregation. Members wore identical black outfits, matching away-team patches, and the same Nike sneakers. From across the room, you knew instantly who was and was not one of them. The uniform made belonging visible.

Brands do the same thing in a much lighter way. The Oura Ring is my favorite version of this: it takes a belief — I take my health seriously — and turns it into a symbol people can see. A few years back, my friend Bob got one before almost anyone. I asked him about this black ring I'd never seen him wear, thinking it was just jewelry, and he told me the whole story — how this ring worked, what it tracked, everything. After that, I started to notice the ring on a lot more people, and about two Christmases later I bought two for my stepsons. If Bob had just been tracking his sleep silently in an app, we never would have talked about it. The symbol started the conversation, and the conversation made the next convert.

This is how Fitbits spread. Whoop. Even Garmin among runners. Apple has always known this: when everyone else's earbuds were black, theirs were white, so you could spot a fellow believer across a subway car. A crucifix does the same job. So does that giant Stanley cup. When you wear a symbol of your belief and belonging, others ask questions, and distribution happens without a dollar of ad spend.

Researchers call this conspicuous consumption and brand prominence. A 2010 study found that visible brand markers signal which group you belong to, and they even earn you better treatment. Sometimes the loudest signal is a quiet one only insiders can read. My friend April rewarded herself with a Rolex. To me it looked like a nice watch. To people who know Rolexes, it told a whole story about the wait list she had to sit on to get it, and what that means about who she is.

The mistake brands make is treating merch as a way to make money instead of a way to recruit. A logo slapped on a mug is not a symbol. A symbol carries a belief, and wearing it says something about who you are. So if you have a digital product or you're a service business, and your customer can't wear or hold a symbol of their allegiance to you, take some time to workshop what you could produce that they can wear or hold. That creates brand allegiance and conversations.

If you're trying to build a brand with a cult following, give your followers one thing they can wear and show off: a color, like Livestrong yellow or Tiffany blue; a phrase, like "you're worth it"; a shape, like the Hershey's Kiss droplet. When you see or share any of these things, you are reinforcing the brand as a real part of your life. Make sure your symbol signals the belief, not just the brand. Another believer must be able to spot it across a room.

## Rituals

We've established that a symbol recruits. The next secret is what keeps people from leaving.

The fourth secret way to drive belief in your brand is rituals. Most people assume belief comes first and behavior follows. It's usually the other way around. A ritual is a formalized series of actions or behaviors performed in a specific, repetitive order — like sage smudging to cleanse the room before meditating.

All Fortune 500 companies do product and feature releases, but Apple turned its yearly keynote into a ritualistic mass. Same time every year, the faithful are watching live. There's the slow build, then the sermon from the prophet, right down to "one more thing." They have disguised a product launch as a holy day for their followers. The luxury brand Hermès runs the opposite version. You do not just buy a Birkin — you have to be allowed to buy one. The wait list is an initiation. You build the relationship. You spend. You wait. You prove yourself. Apple's ritual is an open mass that anyone can attend; the Hermès ritual is a private confirmation you have to earn. Opposite styles, but the same effect: worship from the people, right on schedule.

NXIVM is the dark master of this. On the surface, it sold polished self-improvement workshops to lawyers, actors, executives. But to reach the secret inner circle, you had to pay a steep price to get in — hand over collateral, your most damaging personal secrets, and pass through a painful private initiation. What they revealed about human behavior is that the harder and costlier the door, the more locked in people felt once they got on the other side. Make people work to get in. Cults do it. Great brands with wait lists do it.

This is old science. In 1959, researchers ran a study on initiation and found that people who went through an embarrassing, effortful entry liked the group more than the ones who got in easily. The harder the door, the more you love the room behind it. Your brain reasons, "Well, I suffered to get in, so I've got to make sure it's worth it." A study on rituals in 2013 found that doing a small ritual before eating made a plain carrot taste better and nearly doubled what people would pay for chocolate — but only when they did the ritual themselves, not when they watched it. So when every user experience designer on earth tells you to strip out all the friction, be careful. Zero friction means zero investment, and zero investment means zero devotion.

Build one repeatable thing your people do, and tell them when to do it — like telling coffee snobs to grind coffee beans exactly 11 seconds before starting a pour-over. This is a great way to sell fresh coffee beans and build a tribe of coffee snobs. Then add one earned threshold, one small hoop they jump through to get in. Maybe you sell your coffee beans on Sunday afternoons only, and only to those who've earned the purchasing code — not the coupon code, the code that unlocks the private cart. The bar is the point. Put the rope up and make them work for it to get opened.

## Scripture and Language

The fifth secret of belief is scripture and language. Think of this as the next step to exclusivity, because it makes people not only work to get in, but work to stay in as well.

In 2001, researchers found that a real brand community — one that actually works and isn't just a side note on a content plan — runs on three things: a shared sense of who we are, shared rituals, and a feeling of responsibility to each other. And at the center of it all, shared language.

The mistake is corporate speak: language so clean and generic it could belong to any company in your category. Generic language earns you zero belonging. And if you're not sure that's true — think different.

I'm bringing up Apple throughout because Apple hits each of these points hard and then some. If it did not work, Apple would not do it. Apple has been feeding us language for decades. Think different. A thousand songs in your pocket. There's an app for that. It just works. Anyone inside that world knows every one of those by heart. CrossFit does it even harder: WOD, AMRAP, the box. If you know the words, you're in. If you don't, you're a tourist, and using the language will become part of the act of joining. A psychiatrist, Robert Jay Lifton, studied this in real cults and called it loading the language — tidy in-group terms that quietly do your thinking for you.

A common mistake is coining a phrase with no belief underneath it. Superhuman tried to own "inbox zero," but that phrase was already public and meant nothing new, so it never really stuck to them. A phrase has to carry a worldview that's associated with you from that point on. When it does, it is the cheapest, most viral symbol you can own. Words travel further than a logo, and they convert on contact.

Coin one to three phrases that name something your people feel but could never quite put into words. Recently, mine are "build a church, not a trade school." But 15 years ago I coined "conversion copywriting," which gave a new sense of pride to direct-response copywriters who had always felt second place to creative copywriters. Now half the copywriters on LinkedIn call themselves conversion copywriters, and 10,000 of them have paid us to train them.

Repeat your phrases until people quote them back to you. And when your own team groans that you say it too much, you're just starting to say it enough. Double down, and rename a few ordinary things in your world — because naming is claiming. Name it and you own it.

## The Prophet

By now you've built the lens, the enemy, the symbols, the rituals, and the language. Which means there's only one thing left, and it's the one everything so far has been building toward.

The final secret of getting people to believe in you is the prophet. This is the one you never claim at the start. You earn it at the very top, after everything else is already in place.

You see the best version of that certainty in business. When Steve Jobs becomes *Steve Jobs*, he wasn't the smartest person in the room, but when he walked on stage the lens, the enemy, the symbols, the rituals, and the language were already in place, and you could feel the crowd becoming a congregation around him. Warren Buffett did the same thing. Most announcements for annual meetings go into the spam folder, but people fly in from all over the world for Berkshire Hathaway's annual meeting. And check this out: Warren Buffett stepped down as CEO of Berkshire Hathaway, so 2026 was the first year he didn't run the annual meeting. Did it affect it? It affected attendance. He's such a prophet that without him, attendance was down almost 50% in 2026.

There's research to back this up, too. A 2012 study on the confidence heuristic found that people read confidence as competence, even when the confidence is not earned. When there's no proof yet, certainty is really the only signal we have to go on as humans. So conviction spreads, and people mistake it for being right. We saw the dark version of this with David Koresh — he was dead confident in his vision of the future.

People do need this from leaders, though. Few founders will stand behind their vision. They hedge: "I think this might probably be the future." They sell the future as a maybe. A prophet states the future like it's already here. But you only get to do that after you've completed the previous five steps. If you declare the future on day one with nothing behind you, you're a dude with a cardboard sign on a busy street.

So pick the one thing about the future you actually believe. This is simply living in the future, and it's what all entrepreneurs and founders are supposed to do. Then say it like it's already true. No hedging. Repeat it until your people can finish the sentence for you. And shrink it down to a single line they can pass around, which loops us right back to language.

Do that and you've got yourself a moat — but your moat is also filled with crocodiles, and those crocodiles are the community that pays you to stay involved and that recruits new believers for free.

Great brands build a church, not a trade school.
