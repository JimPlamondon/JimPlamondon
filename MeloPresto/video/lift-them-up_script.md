# "LIFT THEM UP"

**A MeloPresto film.** :70
Draft 2.

---

## The form

One voice. Ten cards. Seventy seconds.

The voiceover **never names anyone and never explains anything**. That is the whole trick of the film we're paying tribute to, and it's why seventeen people fit into a minute there: the narration is a hymn to a *type* of person, and the pictures and name cards carry the who. Draft 1 got this wrong — it gave every innovator an explanatory clause and ran to 2:45. This one runs :70 — ten seconds over the spot we're saluting, because we have ten cards to land — and it gets there by letting the pictures do their share of the work.

Black and white throughout. Ink, manuscript, plate, patent linework. **Color arrives exactly once: the last shot.**

**No portraits. Not one.** The film shows what these people *made*, animated — the staff being drawn, the lattice turning, the accidentals struck out and new lines sliding in between the old ones, the same shape played in two different places on the button field. A face tells you nothing about what someone figured out; the artifact tells you everything, and it moves. This also means we never hunt for a likeness that may not exist, never clear a photograph, and never ask a living colleague to hand over a headshot.

Each card carries three things: **the name, the date, and the contribution in one line.** The voiceover still explains nobody — the card does the attribution while the animation does the argument, and the narration stays a hymn to the type of person.

## Narration — clean read

**128 words**, ~:67 at an unhurried 115 wpm. Unhurried, warm, no salesmanship. Reading *to* someone.

> Here's to the crazy ones.
>
> The monks. The schoolmistresses. The patent clerks. The ones who wouldn't leave music alone.
>
> Who looked at a page of music and saw a bad interface. Who were told the notation was fine, the keyboard was fine, the tuning was settled — and redrew it anyway.
>
> You can ignore them for a century. You can lose their names. The one thing you can't do is un-know what they figured out.
>
> They made music simpler for people they'd never meet, and asked for nothing back.
>
> Because the ones crazy enough to redraw music are the ones who redraw it.
>
> Every Melodian sees farther because they lifted us up.
>
> So pay it forward. Spread the word. Lift somebody up.

## Sound

One drone from 0:00. Each innovator adds a voice to it — by the maintainers' card it's a ten-voice chord built in front of the audience without them noticing. Schoenberg's voice arrives as the piece's only **modulation** rather than a new pitch: the one key change, on the card about key change.

At 0:53, when the color hits, the chord resolves. At 0:58 it stops being score and becomes **diegetic** — that chord is now coming from the instrument the hands are playing.

Composed original. No library cue.

---

## The last shot is the whole product demo

Two people, seven seconds, no feature names, no UI, no voiceover claim. One person plays. A second person watches, reaches in, and plays it correctly the first time.

If that shot works, the film needs no argument. If it doesn't work, no argument saves it. Shoot it first, before anything else is scheduled — everything upstream is decoration on this.

## Assets

Everything on screen is drawn and animated by us. There are no photographs, no portraits, no scanned plates, and therefore nothing to source, clear, or credit as an image. Historical documents are reference for our own draughtsmanship, not material to lift — including Schoenberg's Chart of the Regions, which we build from the relationships rather than lifting from *Structural Functions of Harmony*, because ours has to move and to match the film's ink-and-lattice hand anyway.

Bill Sethares, Andy Milne, and Anthony Prechtl are alive and are colleagues; so are Werner Schweer and Michael Good. Tell them they're in it before it posts — not for permission, and no longer because we need a headshot from them, but because it is a nice thing to be told, and because on the two shots that animate *their* ideas they will spot a wrong detail faster than we will.

The animatic (`lift-them-up_animatic.html`) is the reference for every scene. It plays the film at its real timing; treat its drawings as blocking, not final art.

## Open questions

1. **Roualle de Boisgelou is in, from a tracked file, not the paper.** `Specs/JiMStudent_Spec.md` §3.3 gives "first described by Roualle de Boisgelou in 1764," so the card reads that way — *described*, which is what the spec claims, not *invented*. Worth one look at the paper itself to confirm the two agree before we cut it.
2. **The *Hex Player* year.** Left off the card rather than guessed. `Nomenclature.md` cites Milne & Prechtl without one.
3. **Euler:** 1739 (*Tentamen*) or 1774 for the lattice — use whichever you defend elsewhere in the corpus.
4. **Sethares:** 1993 (*JASA*) is on the card; 1998 (*Tuning, Timbre, Spectrum, Scale*) is the better-known citation.
5. **Wavu.** The house term is on no card here; a first-time viewer has no wavu yet. Say the word and Euler's card becomes *the Tonnetz — the wavu*.
6. **Aside, not about the film — now resolved.** "Sight-Reading Music Theory" is unpublished: submitted to *JMTP* and rejected. `PAPER_INDEX.md` was right; three spec citations asserting *JMTP* 2009 were wrong (`JiMStudent_Spec.md` lines 60 and 488, `JimmyShujaa_Spec.md` line 686). Corrections are staged on `claude/fix-sight-reading-citations` in the `jims` repo, unpushed. None of this touches the film, which carries no citations — and it doesn't touch Roualle de Boisgelou either: rejection isn't refutation, and the 1764 attribution stands on its own.
