# "LIFT THEM UP"

**A MeloPresto film.**
Draft 1 — for review.

---

## 1. Spec

| | |
|---|---|
| **Title** | *Lift Them Up* |
| **Hero runtime** | 2:45 (2:30 body + :15 end card). See §6 for the :60 cutdown, §7 for a :35 social cut. |
| **Format** | 16:9 hero master; 9:16 and 1:1 reframes from the same timeline |
| **Voice** | One narrator. Warm, unhurried, no salesmanship. Reverent, not solemn. Reading *to* someone, not *at* them. Target ~120 words per minute — the script is budgeted at that pace. |
| **Visual thesis** | The film opens in ink-and-parchment monochrome and ends in full MeloPresto color. Color does not arrive as decoration — it arrives **on Newton**, and it never leaves. The last shot is a living MeloPresto score. |
| **Frame** | No faces. No archival portrait montage. This film is about **what these people drew**, not what they looked like. (See §9 — a deliberate creative and legal choice.) |
| **Nature of the homage** | Litany of unnamed heroes in apposition → a turn → a direct charge to the viewer. Cadence borrowed. Words, images, and argument entirely our own. |

**Logline:** Ten gifts, nine hundred years, one instrument. MeloPresto isn't ours — it's theirs, finally assembled.

---

## 2. The line that governs everything

> **Nothing in this film asks you to buy anything. It asks you to pass something on.**

If a cut, a card, or a music choice starts to feel like an ad, it's wrong. Kill it.

## 3. Hero script — 2:45

Per shot: **VO** (spoken) / **VISUAL** (what we see) / **CARD** (on-screen text).

---

### SHOT 1 — 0:00–0:14

*Silence. Then one sustained low tone, alone.*

**VISUAL:** Black. A nib touches paper. A single horizontal line is drawn left to right at the speed of a hand — not a graphic wipe. Ink bleeds into the fiber.

**VO:** *(after the line completes)*
> Every note you have ever read was put there by someone who didn't have to.

---

### SHOT 2 — 0:14–0:22 · Guido

**VISUAL:** Three more lines join the first. A scriptorium in monochrome; neumes float free above the text, then settle onto the staff and lock into place. **UT RE MI FA SOL LA** illuminate one at a time along a hymn's opening lines.

**VO:**
> A monk gave the notes their names, and gave the names a place to stand.

**CARD:** GUIDO OF AREZZO · c. 1025 · *solmization; the staff*

---

### SHOT 3 — 0:22–0:32 · Glover

**VISUAL:** A Norwich schoolroom. Chalk. A ladder of syllables on the wall — **d r m f s l t** — and a teacher's hand moving up it. Cut to children's mouths, in rhythm, singing. There is no instrument in the room. That's the point.

**VO:**
> A schoolmistress put music in the mouths of children — so that singing needed no privilege, only a syllable.

**CARD:** SARAH GLOVER · 1835 · *Norwich sol-fa; movable doh*

---

### SHOT 4 — 0:32–0:39 · Euler

**VISUAL:** A hand-drawn ink diagram: pitch names arranged in a lattice, fifths one way, thirds the other. The lattice turns in 3D; a chord lights up as a compact triangle, then slides — same shape, new position.

**VO:**
> A mathematician measured the distances between tones, and found a lattice underneath.

**CARD:** LEONHARD EULER · 1739 · *the Tonnetz — what MeloPresto calls the wavu*

---

### SHOT 5 — 0:39–0:51 · Schoenberg

**VISUAL:** Euler's lattice is still on screen. Now each little triangle between the pitches brightens, and **collapses to a single point**. The points join up. The pitch-names fade out and key-names fade in — C, G, D, a, e — and the lattice of *notes* has become a lattice of *keys*. Schoenberg's Chart of the Regions, drawn as what it is: the dual of the thing we were just looking at. A modulation then traces across it as a short walk between neighbors.

**VO:**
> A composer turned that lattice inside out and mapped the keys themselves — so a modulation became a distance you could see.

**CARD:** ARNOLD SCHOENBERG · 1954 · *the Chart of the Regions — the lattice's dual*

---

### SHOT 6 — 0:51–1:01 · Newton — ***COLOR ARRIVES***

**VISUAL:** A prism. White light enters in monochrome; the spectrum leaves it in full color and floods the frame. As the band fans out, seven note-names settle onto seven hues. **From this shot forward the film is in color and never returns to monochrome.**

**VO:**
> A physicist split light into seven parts, and insisted he could hear a scale in it.

**CARD:** ISAAC NEWTON · 1704 · *color mapped to the diatonic notes*

---

### SHOT 7 — 1:01–1:14 · The chromatic staff

**VISUAL:** A conventional five-line staff, now in color. A hand strikes out the sharps and flats one by one. Then the staff *grows* — new lines slide in between the old ones until every pitch, black key and white, has a place of its own. The accidentals become unnecessary and vanish.

**VO:**
> Someone whose name we lost drew a staff with a line for every note — done pretending that half of music was an exception.

**CARD:** THE FIRST CHROMATIC STAFF · inventor unknown

---

### SHOT 8 — 1:14–1:27 · Wicki

**VISUAL:** A patent drawing, then the real thing: a field of buttons. A hand plays a short melody. The hand lifts, moves to a completely different region of the field, and plays the identical shape — different key, same fingering. Ghost-trails show the two gestures are geometrically identical.

**VO:**
> A Swiss inventor laid out the buttons so a melody keeps its shape in every key. Learn it once. Play it anywhere.

**CARD:** KASPAR WICKI · 1896 · *the isomorphic note-layout*

---

### SHOT 9 — 1:27–1:36 · Sethares

**VISUAL:** One tone's spectrum, rendered as stacked partials. The partials are dragged; as they move, the scale that sounds consonant with them visibly re-spaces itself in sympathy. Two things we were taught were separate, moving as one mechanism.

**VO:**
> A researcher showed that tuning and timbre are not two subjects. They're one argument, heard twice.

**CARD:** WILLIAM SETHARES · 1993 · *tuning and timbre*

---

### SHOT 10 — 1:36–1:47 · Milne & Prechtl

**VISUAL:** A split screen that resolves into one image: the rows of the button field on the left, the lines of the staff and the lanes of a sequencer on the right. They slide toward each other and register perfectly — one continuous grid running from the instrument, through the page, into the software.

**VO:**
> Two researchers saw that the buttons, the staff, and the sequencer had been saying the same thing all along.

**CARD:** ANDREW MILNE & ANTHONY PRECHTL · *Hex Player* · *one geometry — instrument, page, screen*

---

### SHOT 11 — 1:47–1:58 · The maintainers

**VISUAL:** Scrolling commit logs. A `.musicxml` file opening cleanly in four different programs. A notation editor rendering a score. A contributor graph filling in. No faces anywhere; **usernames** everywhere.

**VO:**
> And thousands you'll never meet, who wrote it all down and gave it away — to people they'd never know.

**CARD:** MUSICXML · MUSESCORE · AND EVERY MAINTAINER WHO GAVE IT AWAY

---

### SHOT 12 — 1:58–2:17 · The turn

*Music drops to near-silence. Hold the beat past comfortable.*

**VISUAL:** All ten images from the film, small, on a grid — then they converge, overlap, and resolve into a single MeloPresto score: chromatic staff, colored notes, isomorphic layout beneath it, all one object.

**VO:**
> None of them were paid to fix music. They fixed it anyway. Seven hundred years, each one handing the next a piece.
>
> MeloPresto is not our idea.
>
> It's theirs. Finally assembled.

**CARD:** *(none — let the score carry it)*

---

### SHOT 13 — 2:17–2:30 · The charge

**VISUAL:** Push in as a real pair of hands begins to play that score. Pull back: a second person is watching over their shoulder. The second person reaches in and plays it too — correctly, immediately, first try. That is the entire product demo. We never say a feature name.

**VO:**
> Every Melodian is standing on a stack of gifts.
>
> So hand one down. Tell someone. Lift them up.

**CARD:** *(hold, then)* **MeloPresto** — *Lift them up.*

---

### END CARD — 2:30–2:45

MeloPresto wordmark. Below it, small, held a beat longer than feels comfortable:

> *Standing on the shoulders of Guido, Glover, Euler, Schoenberg, Newton, Wicki, Sethares, Milne, Prechtl — and everyone who wrote it down and gave it away.*

Then the URL. No call-to-action button. No "Learn more." The film already told them what to do.

## 5. Sound

- **Bed:** a single drone from 0:00, on one pitch. Every innovator adds **one voice** to it. Schoenberg's voice is the one that arrives as a *change of key*, not a new pitch — the only modulation in the piece, on the shot about modulation. By Shot 11 it is a ten-voice chord that has been built in front of the audience without them noticing. This is the film's real argument, made in sound.
- **Shot 6 (Newton):** as color arrives, the drone gains its first genuinely *bright* partial. Sound and picture bloom on the same frame.
- **Shot 12:** cut the bed to almost nothing. The silence is where the audience does the thinking.
- **Shot 13:** the full chord returns — but now it is being *played by a person on screen*, not scored under them. Diegetic. That's the payoff.
- Music is **composed original for this film** (see §9). No library cue picked for sounding like a famous spot.

## 9. Production notes — honoring the homage without borrowing it

The reference is obvious and intended. The homage lives in **cadence and structure only**: a litany of unnamed heroes in apposition, a turn, and a direct charge to the viewer. Everything below is deliberate distance, not timidity.

**Language.** No phrase from the reference spot appears here, and none should be added in revision — not the famous opening toast, not the misfits-and-rebels list, not the round-pegs image, not the closing "crazy enough" construction, and not the two-word imperative tagline or any near-rhyme of it. (*"Play Different"* is off the table: that reads as a riff on a live trademark, not an homage.) Jim's brief used "the crazy ones" as shorthand for a feeling; the script delivers that feeling in our own words — the ones who were told to leave it alone, and didn't. If we want the beat sharper, sharpen it in our vocabulary, not theirs.

**Images.** The reference is a montage of famous faces in archival black and white. Ours has **no faces and no portrait montage**. We show what these people *made*: a staff being drawn, a lattice turning, a prism, a patent drawing, a spectrum, a commit log. Different subject, different argument — and a stronger one for a product about notation. The monochrome-to-color arc is ours and does real work: it *is* the MeloPresto color mapping, demonstrated rather than claimed.

**Tagline.** "Lift them up" comes from Jim's own framing and closes the film's own logic — gifts received, gift passed on. It is not a parody of anything.

**Rights checklist before production:**

> **LAUNCH GATE — before anything else.** The MeloPresto rename-and-registration workstream (`ws_melopresto_rename_registration_20260826`) carries an explicit launch-order constraint: **no public MeloPresto pages or assets until the CNIPA trademark application is submitted.** This film is a public MeloPresto asset with the wordmark on the end card. It can be written, shot, and cut now — it **cannot be posted** until that gate clears. Treat the CNIPA submission as this film's release dependency, and don't let a "soft" upload to an unlisted URL count as an exception.

1. **Living people named on screen** — Sethares, Milne, Prechtl, Schweer, Good, Hayden. Their *work* can be described freely; naming them in a promotional film is a courtesy question and, in some jurisdictions, an endorsement question. **Get written OK from each before locking.** Several are people Jim has published with; a direct note will likely be welcome, and their input may improve the shots. If anyone declines, describe the contribution and drop the name — the film survives it.
   Note that Bill Sethares is already mid-conversation with us on a separate matter — the written grant for the TransFormSynth-derived port in `dt-spectral` (see `PROVENANCE.md`). Do **not** bundle the film-credit ask into that request; a courtesy credit and a licence grant should not arrive in the same email, or the small ask will contaminate the large one.
2. **Historical figures** — Guido, Glover, Euler, Newton, Wicki are long dead and in the public domain. No clearance needed.
3. **Schoenberg is the exception among the historical names.** He died in 1951, but *Structural Functions of Harmony* was published in 1954 and remains in copyright, with rights administered on behalf of the estate. The *idea* — regions arranged as a lattice, the dual of the Tonnetz — is a system, and systems aren't copyrightable. **The specific published diagram is expression.** So: draw our own chart from the underlying relationships, in MeloPresto's own visual language, and do not scan, trace, or re-letter the plate from the book. Same rule for any quotation from the text: don't.
4. **Imagery** — every manuscript, diagram, and patent page must be public domain or our own recreation, with source and status logged per asset. Museum photographs of public-domain documents can still carry their own claims; prefer our own recreations, which also let us art-direct them properly.
5. **Music** — original composition only, work-for-hire or CC0, written to the drone-build spec in §5.
6. **Marks** — MuseScore and MusicXML are other people's names. Use them factually, no logo lockups implying endorsement, and follow each project's trademark and attribution guidance.
7. **Our own claims** — "free and open source" appears in the credits card. Make sure it is true of everything shipping under the MeloPresto name the day this posts.

## 10. Open questions for Jim

1. **The chromatic staff (Shot 7).** The brief refers to "the first known inventor of the chromatic staff." I could not attribute that to a specific person with the confidence the other eight names carry, so the script honors an unnamed inventor — which reads well and is honest. **If you have the name and date, it becomes a named card like the others.** This is the one factual hole in the script.
2. **Euler's date.** Shot 4 uses 1739 (*Tentamen novae theoriae musicae*); the lattice diagram is also cited to his 1774 work. Use whichever you defend in print elsewhere, so the film matches the rest of the corpus.
3. **Sethares' date.** 1993 (the *JASA* paper) is on the card; 1998 (*Tuning, Timbre, Spectrum, Scale*) is the more recognizable citation. Your call.
3a. **The *Hex Player* year.** Shot 10's card carries no date, because I'm not confident of the publication year and would rather leave it blank than guess on a card that will be on screen for ten seconds. `Nomenclature.md` cites Milne & Prechtl without one. Give me the year and it goes on.
3b. **Wavu, or Tonnetz?** The house term is **wavu**; the film's VO says "lattice" throughout and the cards say Tonnetz, because a viewer meeting MeloPresto for the first time has no wavu yet. Euler's card currently splits the difference — *"the Tonnetz — what MeloPresto calls the wavu."* If you'd rather the film teach the house word from the first frame, say so and I'll push wavu into the VO on the Euler and Schoenberg shots.
3c. **Schoenberg earns his shot twice over.** He is the only figure here who contributes a *dual* rather than a new object, which is why his shot reuses Euler's image instead of introducing one — the two shots are a single seven-second argument about the same lattice seen from its other side. If the film has to lose a shot, these two should be cut as a pair or not at all; cutting Euler alone leaves Schoenberg's "that lattice" pointing at nothing.
4. **Hayden.** He is in the credits card but not in picture. Wicki carries the shot as first inventor. If you'd rather the film say "invented twice, independently, ninety years apart," that's a strong extra beat — it costs about five seconds and one more name to clear.
5. **Runtime.** 2:45 is the hero because ten contributions need room. If the placement demands :60, use §6 — and read the note about keeping Glover.
